Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) was a German polymath who made original contributions to metaphysics, mathematics, logic, physics, jurisprudence, linguistics, engineering, and diplomacy — and who conceived, more clearly than anyone before him, the idea of a universal formal calculus in which reasoning itself could be computed.

Born in Leipzig on 1 July 1646, two years before the Peace of Westphalia, he was raised in the library of his father, a professor of moral philosophy who died when the boy was six. By his own account he taught himself Latin and Greek from the shelves, and entered the university at fifteen. He took a bachelor's degree in philosophy at Leipzig, a doctorate in law at Altdorf (declining a professorship there), and spent his early career in the service of the elector of Mainz, where he worked on legal reform and drafted proposals for a European confederation intended to preserve peace among Christian powers.

In 1672 he was sent on a diplomatic mission to Paris — the intellectual capital of Europe — and stayed four years. There he met Christiaan Huygens, who set him problems that redirected his mathematics; studied the unpublished manuscripts of Descartes and Pascal; and, in the autumn of 1675, invented the differential and integral calculus independently of Newton. He published his method in 1684, with the notation — d, ∫, dx — that the world still uses.

In 1676 he entered the service of the dukes of Hanover, where he would remain for forty years — as court counselor, librarian, mining engineer, genealogist, and de facto chief scientist. The position funded his work but demanded his time: he redesigned the Harz silver mines' drainage systems, served as historiographer compiling a history of the House of Guelph, and traveled endlessly across Europe's courts and academies.

His philosophy matured in the 1680s. The Discourse on Metaphysics (1686) and the correspondence it provoked with Antoine Arnauld forced him to sharpen his doctrine of individual substance; the "New System" (1695) made it public; the Théodicée (1710) defended divine justice against Bayle; and around 1714 he compressed the whole system into the ninety paragraphs of the Monadology. Alongside these ran an extraordinary correspondence — over a thousand letters survive — with Arnauld, Clarke, Wolff, the Princess Sophia and her daughter Sophia Charlotte, the Bernoullis, and many others.

He was the first president of the Berlin Society of Sciences (founded 1700, largely on his initiative), a foreign member of the French Académie and the Royal Society, and a tireless advocate of learned societies as engines of collective progress. He proposed academies for Russia and Vienna, a universal encyclopedia of human knowledge, binary arithmetic as the foundation of a new logic, and a "universal characteristic" — a formal language in which every truth could be expressed and, in principle, settled by computation: "Calculemus" — let us calculate.

He died in Hanover on 14 November 1716, aged seventy. His death went almost unmarked by the court he had served for four decades; only his secretary attended the funeral. He left behind roughly a hundred thousand pages of manuscript, most of it still being edited today — a corpus so vast that much of his actual thought reached print only in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
