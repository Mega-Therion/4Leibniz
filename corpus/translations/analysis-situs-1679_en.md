And thus we have set forth the fundamentals of the straight line and circle—namely how these loci can meet: line with line, circle with circle, line with circle, by whose intersections other things are determined. Whence it follows that other matters can also be treated by our calculus.

III. ON ANALYSIS SITUS.

Mathematical Analysis, as it is commonly celebrated, is concerned with magnitude, not situation (site); and thus it belongs directly and immediately to Arithmetic, but is applied to Geometry only by a certain roundabout route. Whence it happens that many things are easily evident from the consideration of Situation which the Algebraic calculus shows with difficulty. To reduce Geometric problems to Algebra—that is, those determined by figures to equations—is not infrequently quite tedious, and again another prolixity and difficulty is needed to return from equation to construction, from Algebra to Geometry; and often by this path constructions emerge that are not very suitable, unless we happily stumble upon certain unforeseen suppositions or assumptions. Descartes himself tacitly confessed this when in Book 3 of his *Geometria* he resolved a certain problem of Pappus. And indeed Algebra, whether numerical or specious (symbolic), adds, subtracts, multiplies, divides, extracts roots, which is entirely arithmetic. For Logistics itself, or the science of magnitude and proportion in general, treats nothing other than general or indeterminate number and these species in it...

...of operating, since magnitude is in truth estimated by a multitude of determined parts, which nevertheless varies while the thing remains the same, according as one or another measure or units are assumed. Whence it is no wonder that the Science of Magnitude in general is a branch of Arithmetic, since it deals with uncertain number.

The Ancients possessed another kind of Analysis, distinct from Algebra, which approaches closer to the consideration of situation, dealing with the Given and the Sought of loci. And to this tends Euclid's little book *On Data*, on which Marinus's Commentary exists. On plane, solid, and linear Loci, work was done both by others and by Apollonius, whose propositions Pappus preserved, whence moderns have restored plane and solid Loci, but in such a way that they seem to have shown truth rather than light upon ancient doctrine. Yet this kind of Analysis neither reduces the matter to a calculus, nor is it carried down to first principles and elements of situation, which is necessary for a perfect Analysis. True Analysis of Situation (Analysis Situs) is therefore still to be supplied; and this is evident if only because all Analysts, whether practicing Algebra in the modern manner or treating data and sought-for items in the ancient format, must assume many things from elementary Geometry which are derived not from magnitude, but from the consideration of figure, and which have hitherto not been accessible by any determined method. Euclid himself was forced to assume certain axioms quite abstrusely without proof, so that other things might proceed. And the demonstration of Theorems and solution of Problems in terms sometimes appears more a work of labor than of method and art, although sometimes the artifice of the process seems suppressed.

Figure in general contains quality or form in addition to quantity; and just as things are equal whose magnitude is the same, so things are similar whose form is the same. And the consideration of similarity or form extends wider than mathematics, being drawn from Metaphysics; yet in mathematics too it has manifold use, and is useful in the algebraic Calculus itself, but most of all similarity is regarded in situations or figures of Geometry. Therefore true geometric Analysis regards not only equalities and proportionalities (which in truth reduce to equalities), but also similarities, and must employ congruences born from the combination of equality and similarity. The reason why Geometers have not sufficiently used the consideration of similarity I judge to be this: that they possessed no general notion of it...

...sufficiently distinct or suited to mathematical disquisitions—by the fault of philosophers who, content with vague definitions equal in obscurity to the defined, especially in first philosophy, make that doctrine sterile and verbose. Thus it is not enough to say that things are similar whose form is the same, unless a general notion of form is again possessed.

I discovered, however, having undertaken an explanation of quality or form, that the matter finally comes to this: that *things are similar which, observed individually, cannot be distinguished*. For quantity can be detected only when actual compresence or application intervenes; quality presents something to the mind which one recognizes in a thing separately and can employ to compare two things, even without actual application intervening by which thing is compared to thing either immediately or mediately through a third as a measure.

Let us imagine two temples or buildings constructed under the rule that nothing can be detected in one that you do not also observe in the other: namely, the material is everywhere the same (white Parian marble, if you please); the proportions of walls, columns, and all else are identical in both; the angles in both are the same or in the same ratio to a right angle. Thus someone led into these two temples with closed eyes, but opened after entry, visiting now one, now the other, will find no indication in them by which to distinguish one from the other. And yet they can differ in magnitude, and thus can be distinguished if viewed simultaneously from the same place, or even (if separated) if a third moved object is compared now with one, now with the other—as if a measure, such as an ell or foot or something else fit for measuring, is applied now to one, now to the other; for only then will a means of distinguishing be given when inequality is detected. The same holds if the viewer's own body or limb (which travels with him from place to place and serves as a measure) is applied to these temples; then different magnitude, and through it a mode of distinguishing, will appear. But if you consider the spectator merely as an eyed mind, situated as if at a point, bringing with it no magnitudes either in fact or imagination, and observing in things only what intellect can attain (such as numbers, proportions, angles), no difference will present itself. Similar, therefore, these temples will be called, because only by co-observation either between themselves or with a third—and least of all when viewed individually and by themselves—could they be distinguished.

This evident, practical, and general description of similarity will serve us in geometric demonstrations, as will presently appear. For we shall call two offered figures similar if nothing can be noted in one viewed individually that is not equally detected in the other. Thus it follows that the same ratio or proportion of constituent parts must exist in both; otherwise, individually and by themselves without co-observation, a difference will appear. But Geometers, lacking a general notion of similarity, defined similar figures by equal corresponding angles, which is a special case and does not open the nature of similarity in general. Thus a circuitous route was needed to prove what from our notion is clear at first glance.

Let us come to examples. It is shown in the Elements that similar or equiangular triangles have proportional sides, and vice versa; but Euclid accomplishes this with many circuitous steps only in Book V, whereas he could have shown it immediately in Book I had he followed our notion. We shall prove first that equiangular triangles are similar. Let there be triangle $ABC$ (fig. 77) and another $LMN$, and let angles $A, B, C$ be respectively equal to $L, M, N$; I say the triangles are similar.

I use this new axiom: *Things which cannot be distinguished from their determinatives (or sufficient data) cannot be distinguished at all*, since all other properties arise from determinatives. Now, given base $BC$ and given angles $B$ and $C$ (and thus angle $A$), triangle $ABC$ is given; likewise given base $MN$ and given angles $M, N$ (and thus angle $L$), triangle $LMN$ is given. But from these sufficient data viewed individually, the triangles cannot be distinguished. For in each, the base and two adjacent angles are given; now base cannot be compared with angles; nothing else remains that can be examined in either triangle from the determinatives viewed individually except the ratio of each given angle to a right or two right angles—that is, the magnitude of the angle itself. Since these are found equal in both, the triangles necessarily cannot be distinguished individually, and are therefore similar. For as a Scholium, although triangles might be distinguished by magnitude, magnitude cannot be known except by co-observation either of both triangles together or each with some measure; but then they would no longer be viewed individually, as required.

Conversely, it is manifest that similar triangles are also equiangular; otherwise if there were an angle such as $A$ in triangle $ABC$ to which...

From this it is also clear that equiangular triangles have proportional sides, and vice versa.

In the same way, at first glance from our direct notion of similarity, it is shown that circles are as the squares of their diameters; which Euclid shows only in Book XII, and indeed by inscribed and circumscribed figures, reducing to absurdity, when no such roundabout methods were needed. Let a circle be described on diameter $AB$ (fig. 78), and let square $CD$ of the diameter be circumscribed about it; and in the same way let a circle be described on diameter $LM$, and square $NO$ of the diameter circumscribed about it. The determination on both sides is similar: circle to circle, square to square, and accommodation of square to circle; therefore (by the above axiom) figures $ABCD$ and $LMNO$ are similar. Therefore (by the definition of similarity), circle $AB$ is to square $CD$ as circle $LM$ is to square $NO$; thus also circle $AB$ is to circle $LM$ as square $CD$ is to square $NO$, as was affirmed. By like reasoning, spheres will be shown to be as the cubes of their diameters. And in general in similar figures, homologous lines, surfaces, and solids will be respectively as the lengths, squares, and cubes of homologous sides—which hitherto was assumed generally rather than demonstrated.

Furthermore, this consideration, which affords such ease in demonstrating truths otherwise demonstrated with difficulty, has opened to us a new kind of calculus, entirely different from the algebraic calculus, new in its characters and in the use of characters and operations. Therefore it pleases us to call it Analysis of Situation (Analysis Situs), because it explains situation directly and immediately, so that figures not drawn are depicted in the mind by characters, and whatever the imagination understands from figures...

IV. ON EUCLID'S ELEMENTS (ΠRΩTA).
To the Definitions of Book I.
I. *A point is that which has no part.* Add: *having situation*. Otherwise an instant of time and the Soul would be a point. Let there be locus $Y$; if whatever is in locus $Y$ is $Y$, $Y$ shall be called a point, such as $A$.
II. *A line is breadthless length.* (1) It should have been defined what length and breadth were, lest the obscure be explained by the equally obscure. I prefer to define thus: *A line is a magnitude whose Section is not a Magnitude.* And this magnitude shall be said to lack breadth, since breadth is nothing other than the quantity of section, while length is that along which section does not occur. Let there be a magnitude whose two parts are $Y$ and $Z$, and their common Section $Y$ and $Z$. If now $Y$ and $Z$ is always a point, the magnitude is a line. I call $Y$ the locus of all points $Y$, $Z$ the locus of all points $Z$, and $Y$ and $Z$ the locus of those points which are simultaneously $Y$ and $Z$, or simultaneously in $y$ and $z$.
(2) A line can also be defined by motion, but then time must be employed. Let $z$ be to $t$, and $T$ be time; $z$ will be a line. ### Historical Commentary

1. **Context and Significance**: Written in 1679 (LH XXXV, 1, 9), *De Analysi Situs* represents Leibniz's foundational statement on geometric characteristique and spatial analysis, sent famously in summary to Christiaan Huygens in September 1679. Leibniz seeks a direct coordinate-free geometry based on congruence ($\simeq$) and similarity rather than algebraic numbers.
2. **Definitional Innovation**: Leibniz defines similarity ($similia$) without relying on angle equality: two figures are similar if, when observed individually, no intrinsic difference can be detected between them. This metaphysical definition allows immediate proofs of fundamental geometric theorems (such as $Euc.\ V.1$ / $VI.4$ on similar triangles or $Euc.\ XII.2$ on circle areas $\propto d^2$) without Eudoxian exhaustion or limits.
3. **Relation to Lean 4 Formalization**: The definitions of congruence, point-loci, and similarity in this text directly inform `Leibniz/AnalysisSitus.lean` and `Leibniz/CalculusSitus.lean` in the 4Leibniz formalization tree.
